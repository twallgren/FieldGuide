Proactive Memory Agents

The hard half of memory is not storage — it is when to speak. A parallel watcher that decides each step whether to inject a reminder, and stays silent otherwise.

Reach for it when
You are attention-bound or quality-bound
Long, unattended sessions where drift is expensive to catch late. It spends cost and speed to buy quality and attention, and this is the regime where that side of the trade is the right one.
It is indefensible when
You are cost-bound
Under a cost-bound regime the second agent's overhead isn't justified. No judgment call required — the overhead is continuous and the benefit is a failure mode you may not hit.
effort: high — what the setup cost consists of
Two things, per the note's own cost reasoning. First, building and running the watcher: a second agent — or, per the TGL alternative below, a small trained classifier — running continuously alongside the main one. Second, the trigger policy itself: either fine-tuning a model, which is the paper's approach (SFT + GRPO on Qwen3.5-27B over a dataset called SETA, to train the injection-timing policy rather than relying on prompting alone), or building and maintaining a bespoke trigger policy of your own. Real one-time build cost, not free.

Why each delta reads the way it does

quality-delta
+1
Their benchmarks, not mine

The paper's own ablations are the evidence: selective proactive injection beat passive retrieval, constant injection, and advisor-only guidance, with real pass@1 gains on two benchmarks. Reported gains were +8.3 pp pass@1 on Terminal-Bench 2.0 and +6.8 pp on τ²-Bench, and held for both weaker and stronger action agents.

Scored researched rather than measured — this is their result, not mine, and it's on their benchmarks, not a Claude Code coding session, so the transfer isn't verified.

The failure mode being addressed is specific. The paper names it behavioral state decay: task requirements, environment facts, prior attempts, diagnoses, and open subgoals get buried mid-context or pushed out of the window entirely, so they stop influencing decisions even though they're still "in" the trajectory somewhere.

This is the point of the technique: it's explicitly designed to catch drift and forgotten-subgoal failures before they cost the human a debugging session or a wasted review, trading machine cost for less of your own vigilance during long tasks.

A standing choice

Nothing here hands you a per-task knob — it's a standing architectural choice (build the watcher or don't), not something switched per task the way reasoning-effort or model routing are.

What the architecture actually is

A parallel, plug-and-play memory agent that runs alongside an unmodified action agent — it doesn't touch the action agent's weights or prompt structure. It maintains a structured memory bank from the recent trajectory and, each step, decides to inject a memory-grounded reminder or stay silent.

Selective/proactive injection beats passive retrieval, constant injection, and advisor-only guidance in their ablations — the timing of the reminder matters as much as its content. This is the paper's real claim: not "add memory" but "add a policy for when to speak."

Claude Code's built-in memory system (CLAUDE.md + auto memory) is not an implementation of this pattern — it's closer to the "passive retrieval" baseline the paper's ablations beat. Per the official docs, verified 4 Aug 2026: CLAUDE.md and auto memory's MEMORY.md (first 200 lines / 25KB) are loaded once, at the start of every session — not selectively injected mid-task based on a live read of whether the agent is currently drifting or repeating a mistake. The closest built-in levers are hooks that fire during a session — PostToolUse, UserPromptSubmit, PreCompact/SessionEnd — but none of these ship a policy for deciding when a reminder is warranted.

Nor do the surveyed frameworks. LangGraph ships a Store with semantic search, Microsoft Agent Framework adds memory via context providers, CrewAI bundles short-term/long-term/entity memory with RAG retrieval, the OpenAI Agents SDK leaves recall entirely to what you bolt on, and Kiro's steering files load into every session the same way CLAUDE.md does. Net: none of them ship the paper's actual mechanism — a trained policy deciding when to interrupt with a reminder — as a first-class feature. All of them solve "where do I store/retrieve memory," which is the easier half of the problem.

Challenges
arxiv 2605.30152
Do Proactive Agents Really Need an LLM to Decide When to Wake and What to Anchor?

Argues the trigger decision this paper's whole architecture is built around — when to intervene — doesn't need an LLM at all. They frame user/agent activity as a structured event stream an OS or harness already maintains in graph form, and train a small Temporal Graph Learning (TGL) model to output a per-event trigger probability and routing score in one forward pass; the LLM is only invoked after the trigger fires, to phrase the handoff. Reported: TGL improves F1 over LLM-as-trigger baselines on 14 backbones (mean +16.7 points) while running roughly 4–7x faster on GPU and 12–83x faster on consumer hardware, with a ~220MiB on-device footprint. This is a direct challenge to this note's paper's SFT+GRPO-tuned-LLM approach to the same "when to speak" problem — same mechanism (selective proactive injection), argued at a much cheaper lever (a small classifier vs. a fine-tuned 27B model as the gate).

Where the score could move
The perspectives entry matters to the vector, not just the framing. If the TGL-style cheap-classifier alternative holds up outside its original (OS-activity-stream) domain, the cost-delta could move toward neutral or positive, since it replaces a fine-tuned-LLM gate with a ~220MiB model — that would push this from "trade" toward "no-regret" for the injection-timing piece specifically, though the memory-bank maintenance itself still costs something regardless. Nothing has been remeasured yet, so the vector above stands as recorded.
